2 Peter 2:1 teaches that Christ died for false teachers who will be destroyed in final judgement, indicated that the atonement is not for the elect alone. Christ died for all people, even those who are not eventually saved.

"But false prophets also appeared among the people, just as there will also be false teachers among you, who will secretly introduce destructive heresies, even denying the Master who bought them, bringing swift destruction upon themselves." (2 Peter 2:1, NASB)

The Master here is identified as "our only Master and Lord, Jesus Christ," in the parallel passage in Jude 4, and the term "bought," or agarazō, is atonement language. This is an atoned person who is not of the elect, showing that Christ did not die only for the elect.

This text is a serious challenge to the Calvinist view limited atonement (or definite atonement), which states that Christ died only for the elect.[1] If this passage truly indicates that Christ bought someone who was eventually destroyed in judgement, then it is false that atonement was for the elect in particular.

Calvinist Response 1: Bought Out of Egypt

An alternative interpretation from Calvinists asserts that Peter is not speaking about matters relating to salvation or atonement at all. Rather, 'the Master' is God the Father in his capacity as creator and sovereign owner, and that the 'purchasing' is analogous to YHWH purchasing the nation of Israel out of slavery. Wayne Grudem states:

"... rebellious Jewish people (or church attenders in the same position as rebellious Jews) who were rightly owned by God because they had been bought out of the land of Egypt (or their forefathers had), but they were ungrateful to him."[2]

This interpretation is textually unjustified. The passage is not talking about being bought out of Egypt; it makes no such reference.

1. Agarazō references atonement.

As the Calvinist Thomas Schreiner points out, the agarazō word group always has a meaning relating to salvation when associated with the death of Christ. He states that this interpretation appears to be special pleading.[3]

James White argues in support of this Calvinist reading, stating that when agarazō is used to reference atonement, it states it as "bought for a price" (e.g. 1Co 6:20). White argues that because there is no phrase 'for a price,' the passage doesn't likely reference atonement. The Lutheran scholar Jordan B. Cooper pointed out that White's claim is simply false.[4] The phrase 'for a price' is not present in Revelation 5:9 and 14:3-4 and still references Christ's work on the cross.

2. Jude 4 parallel.

The books of 2 Peter and Jude parallel one another.[5] This can help determine the meaning of a term in one passage by use of the parallel. When Jude 4 references the same false teachers, it states that they deny "our Master and Lord, Jesus Christ." This confirms that the term "master," despotēs, references Christ. Since the 'bought out of Egypt' interpretation holds that the master is the Father, the interpretation cannot be maintained; Jude reveals the master is Christ.

John Owen17th Century English Puritan church leader, academic, and theologian who wrote a famous work defending limited atonement entitled, The Death of Death in the Death of Christ. stated that "[i]t is uncertain, that 'the Lord' means the Lord Christ; the word in the original is δεςπότης, which is seldom or never ascribed to him."[6] I do not understand how Owen could state that the Greek term despotēs, "seldom or never," references Christ when the parallel passage uses the term in reference to Christ. It's because of this claim that Owen says this proof text is "doubtful." I struggle to see eye to eye with him here.

It's also worth pointing out that despotēs is not the term used for the Father in the Greek translation of Deuteronomy.[7] There are no textual allusions that would have clearly shown to Peter's audience he was referencing the Exodus story.

Calvinist Response 2: Phenomenological Interpretation

Calvinists who reject the 'bought out of Egypt view' might state that Peter is referencing people who appeared to be Christians and appeared to be of those for whom Christ died but were in fact not. This is a phenomenological interpretation, since it interprets the language as applying to how the false teachers would have appeared to be.

While he acknowledges that the non-Calvinist interpretation is "straightforward and clear," the Calvinist scholar Thomas Schriener prefers this phenomenological interpretation. Because the false teachers were initially active, professing members of the visible church, they seemed to have been purchased by Christ's blood. However, their following destruction in judgement proves that they were never, in fact, bought. Schriener relies on both the view that all who are bought will be saved and that all who are saved will persevere until the end.

1. This interpretation is textually unjustified.

The real issue with the view is that it is simply unjustified from a textual standpoint. Robert Picirilli puts it well:

"The Lord Jesus, by His death, bought them. They deny Him. Nothing else is justified by the text. As Sailer observes, 'The Bible as a rule designates men to be what they really are—not what they pretend to be'..."[8]

Peter states a dire warning that false prophets will introduce destructive heresies to his audience. Peter simply gives a fact about these false prophets: they were bought by Christ. It's a peculiar and strained reading to state that Peter was intending to communicate that these people merely seemed as if they were purchased by Christ but in fact were not.

Schriener states that "[a] right understanding of 2 Peter 2:1 actually supports definite atonement, since Christ did not actually buy these false teachers—for if he had, they would have persevered." As previously stated, Schriener's interpretation relies on his commitment to the doctrine of perseverance (rejected by Lutherans, many Baptists, and many Arminians), but also his commitment to the view that God elects all who are atoned for (rejected by moderate-Calvinists[9]). There are no exegetical arguments here to suggest that these people were not really bought by Christ; the text says the opposite, that they were bought.

I understand that every interpreter brings their beliefs into exegesis. The issue is that potentially challenging texts are being explained in such a way that they cannot be evidence against the theological framework. If a statement that a person is atoned for is interpreted to mean that they 'appeared to be atoned for,' then what exactly could count as evidence for unlimited atonement? What could the text say differently to convince Schreiener?

2. Peter's rhetoric.

The phenomenological reading does damage to Peter's point in this passage. Peter could have just condemned false teachers for rejecting Christ and spreading heresy, misleading the church. However, he specifically notes that they were "bought by Christ." Why would he include this detail? The natural answer would be that it intensifies their guilt. They deny the very God, who became a slave, was betrayed, brutally tortured for hours, and shed his blood on a cross for their sake. But, if Schriener is right, God did not give these people an opportunity for salvation, and Christ was not crucified for their sins.

While it's not impossible that Peter would make the point that these teachers deny the Christ who looked as if he died for them, it certainly renders his rhetoric moot.
